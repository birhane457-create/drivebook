Most driving instructor bios are forgettable. They list credentials, mention years of experience, and say something vague about being passionate about road safety. Prospective students read three or four of these in a row and can't remember which was which.

A good bio does something different: it helps a student picture what it would actually be like to learn with you.

## What a Bio Needs to Do

Before you write a single word, understand what your bio is for. A prospective student reading it is asking one question: *can I trust this person to teach me to drive?*

Trust comes from three things:
1. **Credibility** — are you qualified and experienced?
2. **Personality** — will lessons with you be comfortable, not stressful?
3. **Relevance** — do you teach people like me, in areas like mine?

Your bio needs to answer all three, briefly.

## Lead With What Makes You Different

Don't open with "I am a fully qualified driving instructor with X years of experience." Every instructor leads with this. It doesn't differentiate you.

Instead, lead with something specific:

- The type of student you work best with: *"I specialise in helping nervous learners — students who've had bad experiences before or who've been putting off learning for years."*
- A teaching approach: *"I teach in a way that builds genuine confidence, not just test-passing technique."*
- Local knowledge: *"I know every PDA route run from the Midland test centre and I'll make sure you've driven them before your assessment day."*

This immediately tells a student something useful about you that they can't get from your credentials alone.

## Include Your Credentials — But Keep Them Brief

Yes, include your qualifications, how long you've been teaching, and any specific certifications. But keep this section short. One or two sentences.

Students care that you're qualified. They don't need your full professional history.

## Mention Who You Teach Well

Be specific about your ideal student. This isn't about excluding anyone — it's about resonating with the people who are the best fit for you.

If you're particularly good with anxious learners, say so. If you have experience teaching older learners who come to it later in life, mention it. If you've helped dozens of students pass their PDA on the first attempt, include that.

Specificity builds trust. Vague claims ("I'm great with all types of students") build nothing.

## Show a Bit of Personality

You're going to be in a small vehicle with this person for hours at a time. Students want to know you're not going to make that experience miserable.

You don't need to write a stand-up routine. But one genuine, human sentence — your driving philosophy, something you enjoy about teaching, a reason you got into this — makes you feel like a real person.

*"I got into teaching because I remember exactly how it felt to be a nervous learner, and I've never lost sight of that."*

*"I keep lessons relaxed but purposeful — I find students learn better when they're not tense."*

These are small things. They make a real difference to how a student feels reading your profile.

## Mention Your Service Area

Include the suburbs or areas you cover. Students searching for an instructor near them want to know immediately whether you're relevant to them.

Don't just say "Perth" if you're based in Joondalup and mainly service the northern suburbs. Specificity helps you appear in searches for the right area and saves both you and the student from booking a lesson that's an hour's drive from your base.

## Keep It to Three or Four Paragraphs

The instinct is to write more. Resist it. Students don't read long bios — they scan for the signals that answer their question. Four tight paragraphs that say something meaningful are more effective than eight that ramble.

## Review It Regularly

Update your bio when something significant changes — new area, new specialisation, a notable milestone like 500 students. A bio that mentions "2022 rates" in 2026 signals neglect.
