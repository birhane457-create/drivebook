Most learner drivers in Western Australia do the majority of their practice in suburban Perth. But country and regional roads are part of WA's driving environment, and the hazards they present are different enough that dedicated preparation is worth while — particularly if you're planning to drive outside the city after getting your licence.

Your logbook hours should include a range of driving conditions. Country road experience counts and is genuinely useful preparation.

## What Makes Country Roads Different

The surface differences are obvious: higher speed limits, longer distances between towns, fewer traffic lights, less lane marking on some roads. But the hazards that catch drivers off guard are less obvious:

**Fatigue.** Long, straight, featureless roads cause driver fatigue more quickly than varied urban driving. The monotony reduces stimulation and attention drops. On country roads, a momentary lapse of attention at 110 km/h has far more serious consequences than the same lapse at 60 km/h.

**Animals on the road.** Kangaroos, livestock, emus, and other animals present a serious hazard on WA country roads, particularly at dawn, dusk, and at night. A kangaroo on a country road at 100 km/h is a collision that can be fatal.

**Road surface changes.** Country roads vary significantly in quality. Sealed roads can have edges that crumble, potholes, gravel sections, or corrugated surfaces. Some roads narrow unexpectedly.

**Limited emergency services.** If something goes wrong on a country road, response times are longer. This makes vehicle preparation and good driving decisions more important than in an urban environment.

**Road trains and large vehicles.** On WA highways, road trains — trucks with multiple trailers — share the road. They are long, wide, and create significant wind disturbance when overtaking. Passing a road train or being passed by one requires specific technique.

## Speed on Country Roads

The 100 km/h default outside built-up areas is a maximum, not a target. Conditions often warrant lower speed:

- Reduced visibility (dawn, dusk, fog, rain)
- Unfamiliar roads where the alignment ahead is unknown
- Where animals are likely (scrubland, farmland)
- At night

Driving at 100 km/h when you can only see 100 metres ahead means you're travelling at the outer edge of your stopping distance. In darkness on a country road, that's a real risk.

## Overtaking

Overtaking on a two-lane highway requires clear visibility, sufficient speed differential, and confident execution. Half-hearted overtaking is more dangerous than not overtaking at all.

Before overtaking:
- Confirm the road ahead is clear for the full overtaking distance
- Check your mirrors and signal
- Accelerate firmly to complete the overtake in the minimum time
- Return to the left lane promptly after clearing the vehicle ahead
- Never overtake approaching a crest, corner, or intersection

If the opportunity closes (oncoming vehicle appears), abort the overtake and return to your lane early. Don't try to force a closing gap.

## Road Trains

When a road train is approaching from the opposite direction on a narrow road, slow down and move as far left as safely possible. The width and speed of a road train creates a pressure wave that can push a small car if you're close.

When overtaking a road train, pass at a safe speed differential, maintain a straight course, and don't cut back in front until the full length of the road train is visible in your rear-view mirror. Road trains can be 50+ metres long.

## Animals

The most effective approach to animals on country roads:
- Reduce speed at dusk, dawn, and in areas with animal warning signs
- If an animal appears on the road, brake firmly — don't swerve unless braking alone cannot prevent a collision
- Swerving at speed is a cause of serious rollovers
- After passing through an area with one animal, watch for more — they travel in groups

## Building Country Road Experience Into Your Logbook

If you're planning to drive regionally after getting your licence, include at least a few sessions on country or regional roads in your 50 hours. Ask your instructor to take you on a regional route — even 30–40 minutes on a country highway gives you experience that's genuinely different from suburban practice.
