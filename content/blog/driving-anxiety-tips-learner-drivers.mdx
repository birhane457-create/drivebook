Driving anxiety is more common than most learners realise. Sitting behind the wheel of a vehicle, responsible for something that can cause real harm if handled badly, is genuinely stressful. The good news is that anxiety doesn't have to stop you from learning to drive or passing your PDA.

## Why Driving Anxiety Happens

Anxiety when learning to drive often comes from a few specific sources:

**Fear of making mistakes.** Driving mistakes have real consequences, and knowing that is enough to make many learners tense up. The more they think about what could go wrong, the harder it becomes to focus on what they need to do.

**Worrying about other people.** Learner drivers are often self-conscious about holding up traffic, making errors in front of other drivers, or inconveniencing their instructor. This adds a social layer of stress on top of the technical demands.

**Specific situations.** Some learners are fine in quiet streets but panic at roundabouts, on freeways, or in heavy traffic. The anxiety isn't about driving in general — it's about particular scenarios that feel uncontrollable.

**Test anxiety.** The PDA carries real consequences, and the pressure of being assessed can cause learners to perform worse than they do in practice.

## What Anxiety Does to Your Driving

When you're anxious, your body goes into a state that's actually counterproductive for driving. Muscle tension makes steering inputs less smooth. Attention narrows, so you focus on the thing you're anxious about and miss other hazards. Decision-making slows.

Understanding this can help. When you notice anxiety affecting your driving, you're not becoming a worse driver — you're experiencing a normal physiological response that you can manage.

## Practical Strategies That Work

**Slow your breathing.** When you feel anxious, your breathing becomes shallow. Deliberately slowing and deepening your breath for 30 seconds can reduce the physical symptoms of anxiety quickly. You can do this before you start the engine, or paused at a red light.

**Name what's happening.** Saying to yourself — or to your instructor — "I'm feeling anxious about this roundabout" takes some of the power out of the feeling. It also opens a conversation with your instructor about what specifically is worrying you.

**Break situations down.** If roundabouts make you anxious, focus only on the approach: speed, lane, give way rules. Once you're in the roundabout, focus only on exiting. Anxiety thrives on imagining the whole situation at once. Breaking it into individual steps makes each step manageable.

**Build gradually.** If freeways cause anxiety, don't start with the Mitchell Freeway at peak hour. Ask your instructor to take a quieter, shorter freeway section first, then build up. Exposure over time reduces anxiety more reliably than forcing yourself through the worst case.

**Don't white-knuckle through it.** Gripping the steering wheel tightly is a physical manifestation of anxiety that makes your driving worse. Hold the wheel firmly but relaxed. If you notice you're white-knuckling, consciously relax your grip.

## Talking to Your Instructor

A good driving instructor understands anxiety and knows how to work with it. Tell your instructor early on that you experience anxiety, and be specific about which situations are hardest.

This lets them structure lessons to build confidence gradually rather than inadvertently putting you in high-anxiety situations before you're ready. It also means they won't misinterpret nervousness as incompetence.

If you feel your current instructor's teaching style is making your anxiety worse — they're critical rather than constructive, they push you faster than you're comfortable with, or they seem impatient — it's okay to look for a better fit. Instructor-student compatibility matters.

## The Day of Your PDA

Test anxiety is a specific challenge. It's normal to feel more nervous on PDA day than during practice lessons.

A few things that help:
- Sleep well the night before, not the morning of (pulling an all-nighter never helps)
- Eat a proper meal beforehand
- Arrive early so you're not rushed
- Remind yourself that your assessor is not trying to fail you — they want you to demonstrate competence, not perfection
- Use controlled breathing in the car before you start
- Focus on one instruction at a time, not the test as a whole

If you've done the preparation — 50+ hours, regular lessons with quality instruction, specific practice on test routes — your skills are there. The anxiety is usually a gap between how much you know and how much you trust yourself.
