A driving instructor without a cancellation policy is leaving money on the table and setting themselves up for frustration. When a student cancels two hours before a lesson with no consequence, you've lost that income with no chance to fill the slot.

A cancellation policy isn't about being difficult — it's about running a sustainable business. Here's how to set one that works.

## Why You Need a Written Policy

Without a written policy, every cancellation becomes a negotiation. The student assumes they can cancel freely. You feel awkward charging them. You lose income and goodwill at the same time.

A written policy removes the awkwardness entirely. The student agreed to it when they booked. If they cancel late, the charge isn't personal — it's the terms they accepted.

## What a Good Policy Looks Like

Most instructors use a tiered structure based on how much notice is given:

**48+ hours notice:** Full refund or reschedule, no charge.

**24–48 hours notice:** 50% charge, or a credit toward a future lesson.

**Less than 24 hours notice:** Full lesson charge.

**No-show (no contact):** Full lesson charge, and a note on the account.

These are common industry standards in Australia. Students won't find them unreasonable if the policy is communicated clearly upfront.

Some instructors use a slightly different structure — for example, allowing one free late cancellation per student and charging on the second. This can work well if you have a regular student base, but makes more administration work.

## When to Communicate Your Policy

Your cancellation policy should be communicated at three points:

1. **At booking:** Include it in your booking confirmation email or SMS.
2. **In your profile:** List it clearly on your DriveBook profile or booking page.
3. **Before first lesson:** Mention it briefly in conversation — "Just so you know, I have a 24-hour cancellation policy, which I've included in your confirmation."

The goal is that a student can never claim they didn't know. If they've seen it three times before an issue arises, the conversation is straightforward.

## How to Enforce It

This is where many instructors falter. They have a policy but don't apply it, either because they feel bad or because they don't want to create conflict.

If you don't enforce your policy, it doesn't exist.

When a late cancellation occurs, send a polite message: "Thanks for letting me know. As per the cancellation policy you agreed to at booking, the late cancellation fee of [amount] applies. This will be deducted from your wallet balance."

Keep it factual and professional. You're not punishing them — you're applying the terms they agreed to.

For students who dispute a legitimate charge, explain the policy calmly once. If they continue to push back, hold your position. Most students won't escalate further. Those who do are usually not students you want to keep.

## Exceptions: When to Waive

There are situations where waiving a cancellation fee is the right call:

- A genuine emergency (hospitalisation, family bereavement)
- A student who is otherwise reliable and this is a first occurrence
- A situation where you yourself contributed to the confusion

Use your judgement. The policy exists to protect you from chronic late cancellers, not to penalise a good student having a bad week.

Document exceptions so you have a record if patterns develop.

## Prepaid Packages Solve the Problem at the Root

The most effective way to manage cancellations is to require payment before the lesson happens. Prepaid lesson packages, where the student pays for a block of lessons upfront, dramatically reduce no-shows.

When a student has money on account, they have a financial incentive to reschedule rather than simply not show up. DriveBook's wallet system works this way — students load credit before lessons, which means cancellation fees can be deducted automatically from their balance.

This removes the awkward payment conversation entirely and enforces your policy without you having to chase anyone.
