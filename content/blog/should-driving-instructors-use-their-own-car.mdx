One of the practical decisions every driving instructor faces is whether to teach in their own vehicle or to teach in the student's (or their family's) car. Both approaches are used by working instructors, and both have legitimate use cases.

Understanding the trade-offs helps you make the right decision for your circumstances.

## Teaching in Your Own Vehicle

The majority of professional driving instructors in Australia teach from their own vehicle, fitted with dual controls. This is considered the industry standard for professional instruction and comes with significant advantages.

**Safety:** Dual controls — a second brake pedal accessible to the instructor — are the most important safety tool a driving instructor has. In a genuine emergency, you can stop the car. Teaching without dual controls means your only emergency option is grabbing the handbrake or the steering wheel.

**Consistency:** You know your vehicle. You know how it responds, where the blind spots are, how long it takes to stop. Teaching from the same vehicle every day lets you give more precise instruction because you know the car's behaviour intimately.

**Professionalism:** A clearly marked instruction vehicle with L plates and visible instructor branding signals to students and the community that this is a professional service.

**Condition control:** Your vehicle is maintained to a standard you control. You're not discovering that a student's car has bald tyres or a faulty handbrake on the morning of a lesson.

**Tax benefits:** A vehicle used primarily for driving instruction is a business asset with significant deductibility. See the section on vehicle tax deductions in our [tax deductions guide](/blog/tax-deductions-driving-instructors-australia).

The downside is cost. A good instruction vehicle — reliable, safe, comfortable for students — represents a meaningful capital outlay. Add dual control installation, commercial insurance, and ongoing maintenance, and the startup cost is substantial.

## Teaching in a Student's Vehicle

Some instructors teach in the student's own car, typically when the student wants to learn in the vehicle they'll actually be driving for the foreseeable future.

**When this makes sense:**
- The student has an automatic vehicle and you're helping them specifically adapt to their own car
- The student is doing refresher lessons rather than full novice training
- The student has specific needs related to their own adapted vehicle

**The risks:**
- No dual controls. Your safety response is limited to verbal instruction, the handbrake, or physical intervention.
- Unknown vehicle condition. You're dependent on the student's maintenance standards.
- Liability. If the student's vehicle is in poor condition and an incident occurs, the insurance situation is more complex.

**Insurance note:** If you teach in a student's vehicle, check your professional indemnity and public liability insurance covers this scenario. Many instructor policies are written around the assumption that you teach in your own vehicle.

## A Hybrid Approach

Some instructors offer both: they teach primarily from their own vehicle but will conduct occasional lessons in a student's car — particularly for students who want to practise in their specific vehicle before their PDA or for post-licence refresher sessions.

This flexibility can be a selling point for certain student segments, provided the insurance position is clear.

## What Most Students Expect

Most prospective students — particularly complete beginners — expect to learn in the instructor's vehicle with dual controls. If you advertise professional instruction and your profile indicates a teaching vehicle, a student showing up to find they're expected to provide the car themselves will likely be confused.

Be explicit in your profile about how you operate. If you teach in your own vehicle, say so. If you offer both options, describe when each applies.

## The Practical Recommendation

For instructors building a professional business, teaching from your own properly equipped vehicle is the standard for good reasons. The safety argument alone is compelling. The professional appearance and consistency advantages build on it.

The upfront cost is real, but it's a business investment that pays off over time — in student confidence, in your own safety margin, and in the professional reputation you build.
